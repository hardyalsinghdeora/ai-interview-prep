# Engineering management

Engineering management interviews test whether you can get sustained results through other people: hire well, give feedback that changes behaviour, plan around real capacity and keep a team healthy under pressure. Interviewers want specific stories and working systems rather than management theory, and they probe the messy cases, such as a failed hire or a missed commitment. Engineering Manager and Director loops ask most, and senior engineers and tech leads moving towards management meet the same questions in a lighter form. This file covers the people, planning and budget side of the job; technical leadership has its own file below.

## A. Fundamentals — must answer crisply
- [ ] What is an engineering manager accountable for, and how would you tell whether you are doing the job well?
- [ ] What makes a 1:1 useful? Who owns the agenda, how often do you meet, and what do you do when it turns into a status update?
- [ ] How do you structure corrective feedback so it changes behaviour? Give an example of what you would actually say.
- [ ] What should a performance review contain, and what should never come as a surprise when someone reads it?
- [ ] What is a performance improvement plan (PIP) for? When is it the right tool, and when is it only a formality?
- [ ] What does a well-designed interview loop for engineers look like, and what is each stage meant to test?
- [ ] What should a new engineer have done by the end of week one and month one, and how do you know onboarding is working?
- [ ] What is the difference between an objective, a key result and a KPI? Give a good and a bad key result for an engineering team.
- [ ] (SDE) What should you expect from your manager, and how do you raise it when you are not getting it?

## B. Working depth
- [ ] How do you work out whether underperformance comes from skill, motivation, role fit or something outside work, and how does the answer change what you do?
- [ ] How do you run a PIP fairly, covering goals, evidence, check-ins and HR involvement? Which parts depend on local employment law? *(verify)*
- [ ] How do you keep the hiring bar consistent and reduce bias across interviewers, including how you run the debrief?
- [ ] How do you build a case for more headcount, and what do you do when the answer is no?
- [ ] Which signals tell you about team health beyond delivery output, and which of them do you trust least?
- [ ] What are the early signs that someone is about to leave, and what do you do in the 1:1 once you suspect it?
- [ ] How do you calculate a team's real capacity for a quarter, allowing for leave, on-call, interviews, meetings and unplanned work?
- [ ] How do you agree the split between features, reliability and tech debt with product, and how do you defend it when a deadline slips?
- [ ] What does your manager need from you each week, and how do you flag a slipping project early without causing alarm?
- [ ] How do you use delivery metrics such as DORA without turning them into targets or individual scorecards? *(verify)*
- [ ] Two teams both claim, or both refuse, ownership of a shared service. How do you settle it before escalating?

## C. Senior / Principal depth (trade-offs, failure modes, scale, cost)
- [ ] Explain the Team Topologies team types and interaction modes. Where do they help, and where do they fit real organisations badly?
- [ ] How does Conway's law affect the way you draw team boundaries, and how do you decide that a reorganisation is worth its cost?
- [ ] How many direct reports is too many? When do you split a team, and when do you add a layer of management?
- [ ] When would you hire senior, grow junior or use contractors? Compare cost, ramp-up time and risk over two years.
- [ ] How does calibration work, and how do you stop it rewarding the loudest manager or the most visible work?
- [ ] Why do OKRs often fail in engineering organisations? Cover cascading, sandbagging, output-based key results and gaming.
- [ ] What goes into an engineering budget, and how do you trade headcount against cloud spend, tooling and vendors?
- [ ] What is the difference between capital and operating expenditure for engineering work, and why might finance care how your team's time is recorded? *(verify)*
- [ ] What does regretted attrition cost, how do you measure it, and how would you present it to leadership?
- [ ] A strong performer brings a competing offer. When do you counter, and what are the risks for retention and pay fairness?

## D. Scenario & design questions (whiteboard / take-home style)
- [ ] You join as EM of an existing team. What do you do in your first 30, 60 and 90 days, and what do you deliberately leave unchanged?
- [ ] You inherit a team with low morale and two resignations in the last quarter. How do you find the causes, and what do you try first?
- [ ] Your strongest engineer is dismissive in code reviews, and two others have asked to move teams. Walk through what you do.
- [ ] Leadership has promised a date, and your team estimates twice the time available. Walk through the conversation and the options you bring.
- [ ] Another team's roadmap blocks your launch, and its manager will not reprioritise. How do you resolve this, and when do you escalate?
- [ ] After a product pivot, you must reorganise 40 engineers. Design the team structure and explain how you would roll out the change.
- [ ] You are told to cut engineering spend by 15% mid-year. How do you choose what to cut, and what do you protect?

## E. Hands-on checks (things to be able to DO)
- [ ] Produce a hiring scorecard for a senior engineer, with competencies, observable signals for each rating and a clear hire or no-hire rule.
- [ ] Write a team charter covering mission, ownership, ways of working, decision rights and on-call expectations.
- [ ] Write a 30-60-90 day plan for a new EM, with the questions to ask and the people to meet in the first two weeks.
- [ ] Build a one-quarter capacity plan in a spreadsheet, with assumptions you can defend, and map a roadmap onto it.
- [ ] Write a performance review and an outline PIP for a fictional engineer, using dated evidence and measurable goals.
- [ ] Draft team OKRs with baselines and targets, plus a one-page headcount request with its cost and the impact of refusal.

---

**Good answers show:**
- Specific situations with what you did, what changed and how you measured it, including cases that did not go well.
- Systems rather than heroics: repeatable habits for 1:1s, feedback, hiring and planning that survive a busy quarter.
- Early, direct handling of performance problems, with fairness to the person and to the team around them.
- Plans based on real capacity and stated assumptions, with trade-offs made visible to product and leadership.
- Commercial awareness: what the team costs, what it returns and how a headcount or budget decision is justified.
- A clear view of what you would escalate, what you would absorb and how you keep your manager free of surprises.

**Prove it:** Build a small management playbook for a fictional team of eight engineers: a team charter, a 1:1 template, a hiring scorecard, a one-quarter capacity plan, team OKRs and a 30-60-90 day plan. Ask an experienced manager to challenge it as if it were real, then revise it and note the three assumptions that changed.
