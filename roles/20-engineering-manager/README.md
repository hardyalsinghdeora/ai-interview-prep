# Engineering Manager

A people leader who is accountable for a team's results and health: hiring and growing engineers, giving feedback, managing performance, planning around real capacity, and working with product and leadership, while keeping enough technical judgement to ask the right questions.

## The role

**Day to day.** Holding 1:1s; hiring and onboarding; giving feedback and writing performance reviews; planning capacity and agreeing the roadmap with product; tracking delivery and risks; removing blockers and resolving disputes with other teams; reviewing designs and incident follow-ups at a high level; managing budget and headcount requests; and reporting honestly to leadership on progress and problems.

**Common titles.** Engineering Manager (EM), Software Development Manager (SDM, at Amazon and companies that copy its ladder), Team Lead or Tech Lead Manager (TLM, on smaller teams with more hands-on work), Senior Engineering Manager, and Manager of Managers or Director at higher levels. The balance of people, delivery and technical work varies by company, so read the job description closely.

**Who hires for it.** Product companies, SaaS vendors, banks and fintechs, services firms and startups that have grown past the point where founders can manage every engineer. Loop details below are typical or reported, not guaranteed.

**How it differs from neighbouring roles.**

| | Engineering Manager | [Tech Lead](../19-tech-lead/README.md) | [Technical Delivery Manager](../15-technical-delivery-manager/README.md) | [Technical Product Manager](../14-technical-product-manager/README.md) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Main accountability | People, team health and results | Technical direction and delivery of one team | A customer deployment or programme | What gets built, and why |
| Direct reports | Yes | Usually none | Usually none | None |
| Hiring and performance | Owns them | Contributes | Rarely | Rarely |
| Coding share | Little or none, varying by company | Roughly a third to a half | Little or none | Very little |
| Measured by | Team results, retention, growth | Team delivery and system health | Delivery on time and customer outcome | Product outcomes |

**Who it suits.** People who get satisfaction from others' success, handle difficult conversations rather than avoid them, tolerate interruption and ambiguity, and accept results that arrive more slowly and less visibly than shipped code.

**Skills mix.**

| Skill | Weight |
|---|---|
| People management (feedback, performance, career growth) | High |
| Hiring and team building | High |
| Delivery and planning | High |
| Communication and stakeholder management | High |
| Technical judgement and system design | Med (High at infrastructure-heavy companies) |
| Org design and budget | Med (High at senior levels) |
| Hands-on coding | Low to Med |

**Career paths.** Senior Engineering Manager, Director, VP of Engineering or CTO; a move sideways to product or programme management; or a return to Staff Engineer. Moving between management and individual contributor work is normal, and is not a demotion.

## Model answers

### The role and your approach

#### What is an engineering manager accountable for, and how would you tell whether you are doing the job well?

An EM is accountable for getting sustained results through a team. That breaks down into:

- **Results:** the team delivers what matters, with reliable systems.
- **People:** the right people are hired, grown, retained, and managed fairly when things go wrong.
- **Team health:** people have clarity, trust and a sustainable workload.
- **Direction and context:** the team understands why its work matters, and leadership understands what the team needs and what is at risk.

Signs that you are doing it well:
- Commitments are met, or risks are raised early.
- Regretted attrition is low, and engagement scores or 1:1 conversations are healthy.
- Engineers can name how they have grown, and promotions come with clear evidence.
- Hires ramp up well, and the bar holds.
- Product and peer teams want to work with your team.
- The team runs well during your absence.

A manager's output is the output of the team, plus that of neighbouring teams they influence (a framing from Andy Grove's *High Output Management*). Avoid using busyness or your personal contribution as evidence of doing well.

#### How technical should an engineering manager be?

Technical enough to do the job well, which varies by team and level:

- **Spot risk and ask good questions:** "What happens when this queue backs up?" or "What is the rollback plan?"
- **Judge trade-offs and people:** assess design quality and the level of engineers, and notice when a plan seems wrong.
- **Earn credibility:** engineers should trust that you understand their work and its difficulty.
- **Represent the work:** translate technical risk into terms that leadership and product understand.

Practical ways to keep current: read designs and incident reviews, join on-call shadowing, pair occasionally, and try the tools your team uses. Be careful about taking on feature work: a manager with little focus time can become a blocker. If you do write code, choose tooling, small fixes or prototypes that do not depend on you.

Match the answer to the company. A first-line EM on an infrastructure team may need deep expertise, while an EM on a product team, or a manager of managers, may rely more on the team's expertise.

#### Why do you want to manage people rather than stay on the individual contributor track?

**Strong reasons:** you have found real satisfaction in helping others succeed, you have already done some of the work (mentoring, hiring or running projects) and you want to work on team and organisational problems.

**Weak reasons:** wanting a promotion, higher pay, authority or an escape from coding. Note that management is not the only route to seniority, as many companies have a parallel Staff and Principal track.

**Structure for your answer:**
1. A specific moment when you enjoyed multiplying a team's work.
2. Evidence that you can do the work: someone you helped grow, a hiring process you improved, a difficult conversation you handled.
3. An honest statement about what you give up and how you have thought about it, including less coding and slower feedback.

If you have already been a manager, explain what keeps you in the role, and which parts you find hard.

#### You join as EM of an existing team. What do you do in your first 30, 60 and 90 days?

**Days 1–30, listen:**
- Hold 1:1s with every report, your manager, the product manager and key peers. Ask: what is working, what is not, what would you change, and what should I not change?
- Read the roadmap, recent reviews, incident reports and engagement results.
- Understand the systems: walk through the architecture, watch a deployment and shadow on-call.
- Learn the expectations: ask your manager what success looks like in six months.
- Take small actions that build trust, such as unblocking something or following through on a request.

**Days 31–60, form a view and start small changes:**
- Share themes with the team, and test them.
- Set up the basics that are missing, such as regular 1:1s, clear priorities and an agreed way of tracking delivery.
- Fix one or two issues that the team cares about.
- Assess the team fairly, with your manager's help, including who needs support and who is ready for more.

**Days 61–90, set direction:**
- Agree team goals with product and your manager, along with a hiring plan, if needed.
- Begin meaningful changes with the team's input.
- Ask for feedback on yourself.

**Leave unchanged at first:** team structure, working practices that work, and past decisions you do not yet understand. An exception is an urgent issue such as a safety problem, a security risk or harmful behaviour.

### 1:1s, feedback and growth

#### What makes a 1:1 useful?

A 1:1 is the report's meeting, used for things that do not fit anywhere else: career, concerns, feedback and context.

- **Regular:** usually weekly or fortnightly, and rarely cancelled, since cancelling signals low priority. Move it rather than dropping it.
- **Their agenda first:** a shared document, with your items second.
- **Not a status update:** if it becomes one, move status elsewhere and ask better questions.
- **Listen more than talk:** when someone brings a problem, ask whether they want advice or a sounding board.
- **Mix of topics over time:** current work and blockers, career goals (monthly or so), feedback in both directions, and personal wellbeing as far as they want to share.
- **Keep notes and follow up:** trust depends on actions actually happening.

Useful questions:
- "What is taking more energy than it should?"
- "What should be different about how the team works?"
- "What feedback do you have for me?"
- "Which work would you like more of, and less of?"

Adapt to the person: new hires need more time, while experienced engineers may prefer less often but in more depth. Remember that the absence of problems may mean a lack of trust.

#### How do you give corrective feedback that changes behaviour?

Give it soon, in private, specifically, and with a clear request. One simple model is **situation, behaviour, impact**, then a conversation:

- **Situation:** "In Tuesday's design review..."
- **Behaviour:** "...you interrupted a teammate three times, and dismissed their caching proposal without asking about it."
- **Impact:** "They did not speak for the rest of the meeting, and the group lost their input on the part of the system they know best."
- **Ask and listen:** "How did you see that meeting?"
- **Agree the change:** "In the next review, please let people finish and ask at least one question before giving your opinion."
- **Follow up:** notice and mention the improvement, or raise it again if nothing changes.

Common mistakes:
- Waiting for the review cycle.
- Vague comments, such as "be more senior".
- Judging character: "you are arrogant".
- Softening the message so much that the person does not hear it.
- Passing on anonymous complaints without any specifics.

Give positive feedback in the same specific way, so that it does not feel like the opening to criticism. Regular feedback makes hard conversations less of a shock.

#### How do you help an engineer grow towards promotion?

1. **Understand their goals:** not everyone wants promotion now. Ask what they want and why.
2. **Use the ladder:** compare their current work with the next level's expectations together. If no ladder exists, write down expectations with your peers.
3. **Find the gap and the opportunity:** the gap is usually in scope, independence or influence rather than coding skill. Find real work that needs the next level's behaviours.
4. **Support, then step back:** give them a project with room to lead, coach them and let them handle what happens. Give feedback throughout.
5. **Keep evidence:** a running document of work, results and feedback from others, updated by them and you.
6. **Sponsor:** speak for them in rooms they are not in, and make their work visible.
7. **Be honest about timing:** promotion depends on demonstrated, sustained performance and on how the company's process works. Never promise an outcome that is not yours to give.

Check whether opportunities are fair. High-profile work and informal mentoring can go to people like the manager, while essential "glue" work goes unrewarded. Review who gets what, and recognise that work explicitly.

#### How do you coach a new tech lead or first-time manager who is struggling?

**Diagnose first.** Common patterns:
- Continuing to do everything personally and becoming the bottleneck.
- Avoiding difficult conversations.
- Over-controlling, or stepping back too far.
- Losing confidence because results are now slower and less visible.

**Then coach:**
- **Clarify the role:** what they own, what success now looks like, and what they should stop doing.
- **Regular time together:** a dedicated coaching conversation, separate from status.
- **Ask questions before giving answers:** "What options do you see?" helps them build judgement.
- **Practise:** role-play a hard conversation before they have it.
- **Observe and give feedback:** sit in on a meeting, or ask their team for feedback with their knowledge.
- **Protect them while they learn:** adjust scope, step in on real risks, and support them publicly.
- **Connect them with peers** and other mentors, plus training or books where useful.

Set a checkpoint. If the role is not a good fit after genuine support, make it safe to move back. Treating this as a change of direction and not a failure keeps a good engineer on the team.

### Performance management

#### How do you work out why someone is underperforming, and what do you do about it?

Be clear on the gap first: what is expected, what is happening, with examples. Then find the cause, because the response differs.

| Cause | Signs | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Unclear expectations | They are surprised by the feedback; goals are vague | Set clear, written expectations, and check for understanding |
| Skill gap | They try hard, but struggle on specific work | Training, pairing, narrower scope, plus a timeline to improve |
| Motivation or role fit | Used to do well, or the work does not interest them | Talk openly, then change the work or look for a better fit |
| Situation at work | Bad project, team conflict, or poor onboarding | Fix the environment, which may include your own management |
| Something outside work | Sudden change, health or family issues | Offer support and flexibility, and use HR and company policy. Do not pry |
| Behaviour or values | Capable, but harmful to others | Be direct, set clear limits and move quickly if it continues |

Process:
1. Discuss it early and directly, with specific examples.
2. Listen, because you may be missing something.
3. Agree written goals and a timeline, with weekly check-ins.
4. Provide support.
5. Record dates and facts.
6. If there is no improvement, bring in HR and move to the formal process.

Ask how much of this is your own doing. Also remember that the rest of the team sees how long you tolerate a problem.

#### How do you run a performance improvement plan fairly?

A performance improvement plan (PIP) should be a real last chance to improve, and not paperwork for a decision already made. If you have already decided that someone must go, talk to HR about the honest route instead.

**Before:** the person should have already received clear feedback. Nothing in a PIP should be a surprise. Involve HR and legal early, as requirements vary by country, and sometimes by company policy or contract *(verify locally)*.

**Contents:**
- Specific performance gaps, with dated examples.
- Measurable goals, achievable within the timeframe by someone performing at the expected level.
- The timeframe, often 30–90 days *(verify company policy)*.
- The support to be provided, such as pairing, training or fewer distractions.
- Regular check-in dates.
- What happens at the end, in both cases.

**During:**
- Hold weekly check-ins, give honest updates and record them.
- Treat the person with respect, and keep it confidential.
- Provide the promised support.
- Assess against the written goals, not general impressions.

**After:**
- If they meet the goals, say so clearly and continue to support them. Watch that the improvement lasts.
- If they do not, follow the process with HR. Be honest and humane, and offer what you can on references, notice and transition.

Plan for how the team's work will be covered, and what you can properly say to the team.

#### Your strongest engineer is dismissive in code reviews, and two others have asked to move teams. What do you do?

Treat this as a performance problem and not as a personality quirk. Two people asking to leave is a measurable cost.

1. **Gather evidence:** read their review comments, talk to those affected while protecting confidences, and look for patterns.
2. **Talk directly and soon:** use specific examples and the impact on others. "Here are three comments from this month. Two engineers no longer want to submit work to you for review. This is affecting the team's results, and it must change."
3. **Listen:** they may be overloaded, may be frustrated about quality, or may not realise the effect they have. Real quality concerns deserve attention, but do not excuse the behaviour.
4. **Be explicit about expectations:** how someone works is part of their performance at this level. Include it in their goals and review.
5. **Support the change:** coach them on written feedback, ask them to raise issues in conversation rather than in harsh comments, and set team-wide review norms.
6. **Check on the others:** talk to the two engineers, and ask whether they would stay if things changed. Take action on what they raise, and do not make them responsible for fixing it.
7. **Follow through:** review progress often. If the behaviour continues, use consequences: a lower rating, removal from a lead position, a formal process and, if needed, exit.

Keeping a harmful high performer often costs more than losing them, and the team notices which behaviour you are willing to accept. Their expertise may be a risk if they leave, so start spreading knowledge now.

#### What should a performance review contain, and how do you run calibration fairly?

**A review should contain:**
- A summary of the rating or outcome, and the reasoning.
- Results delivered, with evidence of the impact.
- How the person worked: collaboration, ownership and behaviours expected at their level.
- Strengths, and one or two areas for growth, with examples.
- Input from peers and partners.
- Goals for the next period, and how you will support them.

**It should never hold a surprise.** Important feedback should already have been given during the period. Keep notes all year to avoid bias towards recent events.

**Calibration** compares ratings across managers to apply the same standard. To keep it fair:
- Prepare evidence in a set format, and compare against written level expectations rather than against colleagues.
- Separate the person's impact from how visible their work was. Include less visible work, such as mentoring, on-call and incident follow-up.
- Limit the effect of skilled talkers: share written cases in advance, and have the facilitator test claims with "what is the evidence?"
- Watch for bias: compare outcomes across gender, location, remote status and part-time status, and ask about differences. Look for language that is applied unevenly, such as "abrasive" or "not leadership material".
- Know how forced distributions apply at your company, and explain outcomes honestly.
- Bring the result back to the person as your own message, not as "the committee decided".

### Hiring and onboarding

#### Design an interview loop for a senior backend engineer.

Start with a scorecard: define what the role needs, and assign each stage to specific competencies.

| Stage | Length | Tests |
|---|---|---|
| Recruiter screen | 30 minutes | Motivation, logistics, pay range |
| Technical screen | 60 minutes | Practical coding close to real work |
| System design | 60 minutes | Trade-offs, scale, failure handling |
| Practical round (code review or debugging) | 60 minutes | Working with real code, quality judgement |
| Past-project deep dive | 45 minutes | Depth, ownership, decisions |
| Behavioral and collaboration | 45 minutes | Influence, mentoring, conflict, learning |
| Hiring manager | 30–45 minutes | Fit with the team, candidate's questions |

Principles:
- **Same questions per stage,** with rating guides that describe weak, good and strong answers.
- **Independent notes:** interviewers record their rating before the debrief, to avoid anchoring.
- **Trained interviewers:** new interviewers shadow first. Include diverse panels where possible.
- **Clear decision rules:** know which competencies are essential, and who makes the final call.
- **Respect the candidate's time:** keep the loop short, give prompt updates, pay for or limit take-homes, and share what to expect.
- **Measure:** time to hire, offer acceptance rate, candidate feedback and performance of hires at six months.
- **AI tools:** decide what is allowed, tell candidates clearly, and adapt the exercises accordingly *(verify current practice)*.

#### How do you keep the hiring bar consistent and reduce bias?

- **Define the bar in writing:** a scorecard with observable signals for each level.
- **Use structured interviews:** the same question sets and rating scales. Structured interviews generally predict job performance better than unstructured ones.
- **Separate observation from conclusion:** notes should record what the candidate said and did.
- **Debrief well:** everyone submits a rating first, then the most junior speaks first, and the discussion centres on evidence. Challenge phrases such as "not a culture fit" or "I just did not click with them" by asking what was observed.
- **Train and calibrate:** run shadow interviews, and compare interviewers' scores with outcomes. Retrain or remove outliers.
- **Examine the funnel:** look at where candidates from different groups drop out, and whether a stage screens out people without predicting performance.
- **Widen sourcing:** do not rely only on referrals, since they reflect existing networks.
- **Hold the line under pressure:** a bad hire costs more than a slower search. Have someone independent review decisions, such as a bar raiser, if the company has that role.
- **Avoid proxy signals:** brand-name schools or past employers, or speaking style. Make reasonable adjustments when candidates need them.

Check local law about what you may ask and record *(verify)*.

#### How do you build a case for more headcount, and what do you do if the answer is no?

Frame it as a business decision, with options:

1. **Goal:** what the business wants that the team cannot do today.
2. **Current capacity and use:** real data on capacity, on-call load, support and project allocation.
3. **The gap:** which outcomes are at risk, in terms of revenue, customers, compliance or reliability.
4. **The request:** the roles, the levels and when they would start, with the full cost including salary, benefits, tools and ramp-up time.
5. **Expected return:** what will be delivered, and by when.
6. **Alternatives considered:** automation, removing work, moving people, contractors or vendors.
7. **Impact of refusal:** what will be dropped or delayed. State this calmly, not as a threat.

Remember that adding people takes time: hiring can take months, new people take time to become productive, and the team spends time on interviews and onboarding.

**If the answer is no:**
- Ask what would change the decision, and what the company's constraints are.
- Agree explicitly what will not be done, and write it down.
- Reduce the load: stop low-value work, automate, simplify systems.
- Review priorities with product, and tell the team the decision and why.
- Report on the real effects, without blame, and revisit next cycle.

#### When would you hire senior, grow junior or use contractors?

| | Senior hire | Junior hire and growth | Contractors |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost | High salary | Lower salary, plus mentoring time | High rate, no long-term cost |
| Time to contribute | Weeks to a few months | Many months | Quick, if the work is well defined |
| Best for | Ambiguous work, or a team lacking experience | Well-established teams with capacity to mentor | Temporary peaks, or specific skills |
| Risks | Hard to find; may leave if underused | Too many juniors can slow delivery and hurt quality | Knowledge leaves with them; legal rules on classification *(verify)* |
| Long-term value | Raises team standards | Builds loyalty and a pipeline for the future | Low |

Consider team balance: a useful rule of thumb is to have enough senior engineers to mentor those more junior, without so many that all interesting work is contested. Consider the two-year picture too. A team made up only of seniors can become expensive and competitive, and one made up only of juniors can struggle with ambiguity and incidents.

Contractors work best on well-defined work with an internal owner. Avoid giving them critical knowledge that you cannot retain. AI tools may be changing some entry-level work, but teams still need a pipeline of future seniors, so check how the company thinks about this.

#### What should a new engineer have done by the end of week one and month one?

**Before day one:** laptop and access ready, a named buddy, a starter task chosen and a welcome note sent.

**Week one:**
- Development environment working, with first code merged and deployed to production, however small.
- Met the team, buddy, manager and product manager.
- Understands what the team does, the main systems and how work moves from idea to production.
- Knows where to ask questions, and has clear expectations for the first 30 days.

**Month one:**
- Delivered a small feature or a meaningful fix with limited help.
- Reviewing others' code, and has shadowed on-call.
- Can explain the main architecture and data flow.
- Has had regular 1:1s and early feedback in both directions.

**Measuring whether onboarding works:**
- Time to first merged change and to first independently delivered feature.
- A short survey at 30 and 90 days.
- Ask each new hire to fix one thing in the onboarding guide. They see gaps that others cannot.
- Track whether new hires are performing at expected levels after six months.

Adjust for seniority: senior hires also need to learn the context and politics of the organisation, and should listen before proposing major change.

### Retention and team health

#### You inherit a team with low morale and two resignations in the last quarter. What do you do?

Listen first, and resist the urge to announce a fix.

1. **Gather information:**
   - 1:1s with everyone. Ask what makes the work hard, what they would change, and what would make them leave or stay.
   - Read exit notes, and with HR's help, talk to those who left if appropriate.
   - Check data: engagement surveys, on-call load, hours, incident counts, promotion history and pay relative to the market.
2. **Identify themes.** Common causes:
   - Unclear direction, or constant reprioritisation.
   - Unsustainable on-call or deadline pressure.
   - Lack of career progress, or a sense of unfairness.
   - A harmful person on the team or a manager who is not trusted.
   - Pay below market.
   - Painful tools, or a lack of autonomy.
3. **Share what you heard:** repeat it back to the team, and be open about what you can and cannot change.
4. **Get quick wins:** fix one or two visible irritations within weeks, and follow through. Trust grows from promises kept.
5. **Deal with the root causes:** create a plan with owners, and give it time.
6. **Protect people at risk of leaving:** talk to those you most depend on, and be honest about what is changing.
7. **Report upward:** tell your manager what you have found and what you need, particularly on pay or scope.
8. **Measure progress:** regular short surveys, attrition and how people talk in 1:1s.

Do not rely on team outings or perks to solve structural problems.

#### What are the early signs that someone may leave, and how do you respond to a competing offer?

**Possible signs:** quieter in meetings, less initiative, fewer long-term conversations, shorter 1:1s, less care about outcomes, changes in hours or more time off at short notice, or recent disappointments such as a missed promotion or a colleague leaving. These are clues and not proof.

**In the 1:1:**
- Ask directly and with care: "You have seemed less engaged lately. What has changed?"
- Ask stay questions regularly, not just when worried: "What keeps you here? What might make you leave?"
- Do not pressure them or react badly. If you penalise honesty, people stop telling you the truth.
- Act on what you can change, and give an honest answer on what you cannot.

**Competing offer:**
1. **Understand what they want:** money may not be the whole story. Scope, manager, growth and stability may matter more.
2. **Decide on the merits:** how critical are they, and are they paid fairly compared with their peers?
3. **Be careful with counter-offers:**
   - A counter-offer can reward people for getting offers, and can damage pay fairness.
   - Many people who accept one still leave soon afterwards, if the original reasons remain.
   - If the person was underpaid, correct that because it is fair, and review similar cases.
4. **Check policy:** work with HR, and stay within pay bands.
5. **If they leave:** make it a good departure. Say thank you, plan the handover, and hold a real exit conversation. Good people may come back.

#### What does regretted attrition cost, and how would you present it to leadership?

Regretted attrition counts the departures you would have prevented if you could. Estimates of replacement cost often range from about half to twice annual salary, depending on role and method *(verify; treat these as rough)*. It is better to build your own estimate.

**Costs to count:**
- **Hiring:** recruiter or agency fees, interview hours and any sign-on costs.
- **Vacancy:** delayed roadmap items, or contractor cover.
- **Ramp-up:** months before the new hire is fully productive, plus mentoring time.
- **Knowledge loss:** incidents and slower work on systems that the person knew.
- **Knock-on effects:** extra load on those left, and further resignations.

**Worked example (invented figures):** an engineer costing 100 units a year leaves.
- Hiring: 20 units for fees and interview time.
- Vacancy: four months at half the value of their work, about 17 units.
- Ramp-up: three months at half productivity, plus a mentor's time, about 17 units.
- Total: about 54 units per departure, or more if it triggers further resignations.

**Presenting it:**
1. Trend: regretted departures by quarter, and compared with other teams or benchmarks where available.
2. Cost: a simple model using company numbers, reviewed with finance.
3. Causes: from exit interviews and 1:1s, with evidence.
4. Proposal: specific actions, such as a pay adjustment or an on-call change, with cost and expected benefit.
5. Measure: what you will report next quarter.

#### How do you measure team health beyond delivery output?

Use several signals, because each can mislead by itself:

| Signal | What it shows | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| Regular short survey | Clarity, workload, psychological safety, trust | Trust matters; answers may be less candid in small teams |
| 1:1 conversations | The real concerns behind the numbers | Depends on your relationship and your own bias |
| Retention and transfer requests | Actual decisions | Lagging: by then it may be too late |
| On-call load and out-of-hours pages | Sustainability | Must be normalised by team and service |
| Leave taken, and evenings or weekends worked | Burnout risk | Sensitive; use carefully and in aggregate |
| Retrospective actions completed | Whether improvements actually happen | Easy to game |
| Participation in discussions | Whether all voices are heard | Personal style differs |
| Peer and cross-team feedback | How others experience the team | May reflect politics |

Psychological safety is a useful focus: do people admit mistakes, ask for help, and disagree with you? A very quiet team may be a warning sign, not a healthy one.

The signal to trust least alone is any activity metric, such as commits or hours. Act on trends, and tell the team what you did with what they told you, or they will stop answering.

### Planning and delivery

#### How do you calculate a team's real capacity for a quarter?

Start from available days, then subtract the known costs. For example, 6 engineers over a 13-week quarter (65 working days each):

| Step | Person-days | Remaining |
|---|---|---|
| Nominal: 6 × 65 | | 390 |
| Leave and public holidays: 6 days each | −36 | 354 |
| On-call: one engineer at half capacity at any time | −32 | 322 |
| Meetings, reviews, interviews, mentoring: about 20% | −64 | 258 |
| Unplanned work and incidents: about 15% | −39 | 219 |

Roughly 219 days are available for planned work, or 56% of nominal. Use the team's own data for these percentages, because they vary a lot.

Then:
- **Adjust for ramp-up:** new joiners contribute little at first and take up others' time.
- **Allocate by category:** for example, features, reliability and debt.
- **Plan to less than 100%:** commit to about 80% of the remaining capacity, to leave room for surprises.
- **Check against throughput:** compare with what the team actually delivered in recent quarters. Past delivery is more reliable than a calculation.
- **Share assumptions:** when someone asks for more, the trade-off is then visible, and changes in assumptions explain changes in plans.

#### How do you agree the split between features, reliability and tech debt with product?

Agree it as a shared decision on the basis of evidence, not as a fixed percentage or a yearly fight.

1. **Show the current state:** how time is being spent, including unplanned work, plus SLO compliance, incident counts and how long typical changes take.
2. **Tie engineering work to product outcomes:** "This work reduces outages in checkout" or "This cuts delivery time for the next three features".
3. **Agree a starting split:** a common starting point is about 70% features and 30% for reliability and debt together. Adjust it to the system's condition, since the figure is only a guide.
4. **Set triggers:** an error budget policy gives an agreed rule, so that when the budget runs out, reliability work takes priority.
5. **Make it visible:** one roadmap containing all work, reviewed regularly.
6. **Revisit quarterly:** the split should change with the situation, such as a launch or a period of instability.

**When a deadline slips:** the temptation is to take from reliability first. Show what that would cost, and offer other options. A short-term shift can be fine if you both agree it, write it down and pay it back. Repeatedly borrowing leads to incidents and slower delivery. A good partnership means product also cares about system health, and engineering cares about business deadlines.

#### Leadership has promised a date, and your team estimates twice the time available. What do you do?

1. **Check the estimate:** review it with the team. Test assumptions, and find out what drives it. Make sure it is not padded or missing anything.
2. **Understand the commitment:** who was promised what, and why this date? Is the driver a contract, an event, or just an internal hope? What is the core need?
3. **Prepare options,** each with its consequence:
   - **Reduce scope:** the smallest version that meets the real need by the date. This is usually the best option.
   - **Phase it:** core features by the date, with the rest to follow.
   - **Move the date.**
   - **Change the approach:** buy a component, reuse something or simplify a requirement.
   - **Add people:** only if the work can be split, and early enough. Adding people to a late project can make it later (Brooks's law).
   - **Accept more risk:** spell out what risk you mean, who accepts it, and do not treat it as free.
4. **Have the conversation early, privately and with a recommendation:** "Here is what we can do by that date, here is what it would take to do everything, and this is the option I recommend."
5. **Be clear about what you will not do:** do not quietly accept the date and hope, and do not make the team work unsustainable hours as the plan.
6. **Record the decision,** track the risks, and give regular updates on progress.
7. **Fix the cause:** agree that future external dates will be checked with engineering first.

#### Your team has missed three deadlines in a row. How do you diagnose it and recover?

**Diagnose with data, and with the team, without blame:**
- Compare the original plan with what happened for each project: when did the slip begin, and why?
- Look for patterns:
  - **Estimation:** consistently optimistic, or based on no data.
  - **Scope:** changes after commitment.
  - **Interruptions:** on-call, support or urgent requests.
  - **Dependencies:** waits on other teams.
  - **Technical:** slow CI, fragile code or tech debt.
  - **People:** key departures, skill gaps or too many projects at once.
  - **Commitment process:** dates set before the work was understood.

**Recover:**
1. **Be honest with stakeholders:** report what happened, what you have learned and what changes.
2. **Re-plan the current work:** cut scope, and give a realistic range.
3. **Reduce work in progress:** finish fewer things, one after another.
4. **Change how commitments are made:** the team has input, there are ranges, and milestones are short enough to show problems early.
5. **Protect the team:** one person handles interrupts, and priorities come through one route.
6. **Fix the top technical bottleneck,** if there is one.
7. **Rebuild trust with small wins:** meet several small commitments before making a big promise.
8. **Support morale:** repeated failure hurts people, and a change in direction needs their input.

Report on a small number of measures, such as predictability against commitment and cycle time, until confidence returns.

#### Another team's roadmap blocks your launch, and its manager will not reprioritise. What do you do?

1. **Understand their position:** talk directly, and find out what they have committed to and why. Their priorities may be just as valid.
2. **Explain the shared stakes:** the business impact of your launch, with numbers. Share what you actually need, and how soon.
3. **Reduce the ask:** can the requirement be narrower, or can a temporary workaround do the job?
4. **Offer help:**
   - Lend your engineers, with their code owners reviewing the change.
   - Pair on the change, or provide the design and tests.
   - Take something from their list in exchange.
5. **Look for alternatives:** change your design to avoid the dependency, or launch without that piece.
6. **Escalate together, if needed:** this is not a failure. Write a short shared summary: the goal, the options and each team's cost. Take it jointly to the person who can make the trade-off. Tell the other manager before you go.
7. **Accept the decision,** and update your plan and stakeholders.

Afterwards, deal with the pattern: identify dependencies during planning, and build relationships with peer managers before you need something from them. An escalation goes more smoothly with someone who already trusts you.

#### How do you use delivery metrics without turning them into targets?

Use the metrics to ask better questions. The [DORA](../../cheatsheets/engineering-management.md#dora-vs-space) measures cover speed and stability: deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate and recovery time *(verify current definitions)*. The SPACE framework adds a wider view, including satisfaction and collaboration.

Rules that help:
- **Team level only:** never use them for individual ratings or stack ranking.
- **Show them to the team first,** and let the team explain them.
- **Follow trends,** and do not compare teams doing different work.
- **Use balancing pairs:** speed with stability, and output with engineer satisfaction.
- **Connect them to a question:** "Why has lead time risen?" might lead to slow reviews, which might lead to a practical fix.
- **Set goals for outcomes:** goals such as "reduce checkout incidents" are better than "deploy twice a day".
- **Watch for gaming:** smaller, meaningless pull requests, or reclassified incidents.

When leadership asks for one productivity number, explain what it would distort, and offer a small set of measures plus a description of business results. Lines of code, commit counts and story points completed are particularly weak measures, and AI tools make volume-based metrics even less meaningful.

#### What makes OKRs work or fail for an engineering team?

**An objective** is a qualitative direction. **A key result** is a measurable outcome that shows you got there. **A KPI** is an ongoing health measure that you keep watching. See [OKR vs KPI](../../cheatsheets/engineering-management.md#okr-vs-kpi).

Example:
- **Objective:** make checkout dependable for customers.
- **Good key result:** reduce failed checkouts from 2.0% to 0.5%.
- **Bad key result:** migrate the payment service to Kubernetes. That is an activity: it might help, but it may not lead to the result.

**Why they fail:**
- Key results that are lists of projects.
- Too many of them, so nothing is really a priority.
- Strict cascading from the top, which takes months and leaves teams without input.
- Sandbagging: setting easy targets when goals are tied to pay.
- No baseline, so progress cannot be measured.
- Set in January, forgotten by March.
- Each team has separate goals although the outcome requires several teams working together.
- Ignoring work that keeps the system running, so it competes unfairly.

**What helps:**
- Three to five objectives at most, with key results based on a baseline.
- Set them with the team, linked to company goals.
- Short regular reviews, ideally every two weeks, and honest scoring.
- Separate them from individual performance ratings.
- Space for keep-the-lights-on work, tracked through KPIs.
- Be willing to drop them if the company's approach does not make them useful.

### Org design and budget

#### Explain Team Topologies and Conway's law, and how they affect team boundaries.

**Conway's law:** organisations tend to design systems that mirror their communication structures. If two teams must work closely, their systems tend to become tightly connected. This also works in reverse, an idea called the inverse Conway manoeuvre: shape teams to encourage the architecture you want.

**Team Topologies** (by Matthew Skelton and Manuel Pais) describes four team types:
- **Stream-aligned:** delivers end to end for a flow of business value. This should be the most common type.
- **Platform:** provides internal services that reduce the load on stream-aligned teams.
- **Enabling:** helps other teams build a capability, then moves on.
- **Complicated subsystem:** owns a part that needs specialist expertise.

And three interaction modes:
- **Collaboration:** work closely together for a limited time.
- **X-as-a-Service:** use a clear interface with minimal coordination.
- **Facilitating:** coach and help others improve.

**Using it to draw boundaries:**
- Minimise handoffs: can a team deliver value without waiting for others?
- Respect cognitive load: a team that owns too much cannot do it all well.
- Match boundaries to business areas and to how often things change together.

**Limits:** real organisations have shared services, on-call needs, specialist shortages, and historical systems. The model is a thinking tool, not a chart to copy. A reorganisation has a cost, so try changing interaction modes or ownership first.

#### How many direct reports is too many, and when do you split a team?

A common range for a first-line EM is about five to ten reports *(rule of thumb)*. The right number depends on:
- The experience of the team: experienced engineers need less day-to-day attention.
- The manager's other duties, such as hands-on work or several projects.
- How varied the work is, and how far people are spread across time zones.
- How many new hires or performance issues there are.

**Signs of too many:** 1:1s get cancelled or shortened, feedback is thin, and problems surface late. **Signs of too few:** the manager has too little to do, and either micromanages or begins doing the team's technical work.

**When to split a team:**
- More than about 8–10 engineers, with meetings and coordination becoming slow.
- Two clear areas of work with different goals, and low dependency between them.
- Too much for one team to own, and quality is suffering.
- Separate leaders are ready to run each part.

**When not to split:** if the result would create two weak teams, or if heavy dependencies would remain between them. Check that each resulting team has enough senior people and on-call depth.

**When to add a layer:** when a manager's span becomes too large even after other fixes, and a person is ready. Each new layer slows communication, so have a good reason. Teams of five or fewer are often not worth a full-time manager, so a tech lead manager could suit that scale.

#### After a product pivot, you must reorganise 40 engineers. How do you design and roll out the change?

**Design:**
1. **Start with the goal:** what is the new strategy, and which outcomes must the organisation deliver? Do not begin with names on a chart.
2. **Map the work:** new priorities, existing systems that still need ownership, on-call and work to retire.
3. **Draft team boundaries:** for example, 5–6 teams of 6–8, with clear missions, minimal dependencies and a platform or shared team if justified.
4. **Test the design:** walk through real projects and incidents. Who would do what? Where are handoffs? Does every system have an owner?
5. **Assign people last:** consider skills, growth and preferences, where it is possible to ask. Make sure each team has leadership and senior capacity.
6. **Check with HR and legal,** especially if roles, managers or terms change *(verify locally)*.

**Roll-out:**
1. **Tell affected managers and senior engineers first,** in a small group with a tight timeline. Avoid long periods of rumours.
2. **Announce it to everyone clearly:** why, what changes, what does not change, who is affected, and when.
3. **Hold 1:1s immediately** with affected people: "Here is your new team, and this is why." Listen to objections, and allow a process for concerns.
4. **Transfer ownership deliberately:** systems, on-call, documents, access and in-progress work. Handover takes planning.
5. **Reset:** team charters, working agreements and new goals.
6. **Expect a dip:** productivity falls for a while, so adjust commitments and tell leadership in advance.
7. **Review after 90 days:** what is working, what is not, and how people feel. Make minor changes without another reorganisation.

Reorganise rarely, and only when the benefit clearly outweighs the disruption.

#### You are told to cut engineering spend by 15% mid-year. What do you do?

1. **Understand the request:** is it a cash, headcount or margin target? Is it this year or the annual run rate? Which costs are in scope, and what is the timeline?
2. **Know your costs:** salaries, contractors, cloud, tools and vendors, plus what can actually change this year. Long-term contracts and severance may limit savings.
3. **Rank options, starting with what does least harm:**
   - **Cloud:** remove waste, right-size, use commitments, set storage lifecycles, and reduce log retention and environments that run all the time.
   - **Tools and vendors:** remove duplicates and unused licences, and renegotiate.
   - **Contractors and agencies:** reduce or bring work in-house.
   - **Hiring:** freeze open roles, and do not automatically replace people who leave.
   - **Projects:** stop low-value work, and reduce scope.
   - **Headcount:** the last option, and only with HR and leadership, following proper process *(verify locally)*.
4. **Protect:** security and compliance, the main revenue systems, on-call sustainability and your most critical skills.
5. **Present options:** show savings, timing, risk and what will stop for each option, together with a recommendation. Show which business outcomes change.
6. **Communicate honestly:** tell the team what you can, when you can. Avoid promising there will be no further cuts.
7. **Follow through:** track actual savings and watch for side effects such as overloaded on-call or rising attrition.

#### What goes into an engineering budget, and why might finance care how your team's time is recorded?

**Budget items:**
- **People:** salaries, bonus, equity, benefits, payroll taxes, recruitment, relocation and training.
- **Contractors and agencies.**
- **Cloud and infrastructure:** often the second-largest item, and it grows with usage.
- **Software and tools:** developer tools, monitoring, security, and AI tools or tokens.
- **Vendors and services:** audits, penetration tests and support.
- **Other:** hardware, travel and events.

Trade-offs are real: an expensive hire might be justified by cloud savings, and buying a vendor product may avoid hiring. Compare by cost per outcome, across years.

**Capital and operating expenditure (capex and opex):**
- **Opex** is charged as an expense in the period it occurs, such as maintenance, support and most SaaS fees.
- **Capex** is recorded as an asset and spread over several years. Some costs of building new software may qualify, depending on accounting and tax rules.

Finance may therefore ask engineers to record time spent on new features or maintenance, because this affects reported profit and possibly tax. Rules differ by country and change over time, so follow finance's guidance *(verify)*.

For an EM, this means knowing the rules at your company, keeping time records accurate and low-effort, and never adjusting them to make numbers look better. See [Cloud budget and FinOps](../../cheatsheets/engineering-management.md#cloud-budget-and-finops).

### Technical judgement

#### How do you approach a system design interview as an EM?

Use the same method as engineers, and check beforehand how deep the interview goes. Interviewers usually check whether you can reason soundly and lead technical decisions, and they do not usually expect you to know every detail.

- **Run it clearly:** requirements, estimates, data model, high-level design, deep dive and operations. See the [follow-up questions](../../topics/03-system-design/ANSWERS.md#d-follow-up-questions-to-expect).
- **Show good judgement on trade-offs:** choose simple, explain why, and say when you would choose differently.
- **Go deep on something real:** one part of the design that you have actually worked with.
- **Add the manager's view without using it to avoid the technical questions:**
  - What can this team build and run, given its skills?
  - What ships first, and how would you stage the work?
  - How would you organise teams around it?
  - What is the cost, and what are the on-call implications?
  - How would you reduce risk during the migration?
- **Be honest about limits:** "I would rely on the database specialists to validate this, and here is what I would ask them."

Some companies will expect you to be as technical as a senior engineer, so ask your recruiter what to expect. Keep your skills up by practising designs on a timer, not just reading them.

#### How do you evaluate a technical proposal when you are no longer the expert?

Ask questions that reveal the quality of thinking:

- **Problem:** what problem does this solve, and for whom? What happens if we do nothing?
- **Options:** what alternatives were considered, including the simplest one?
- **Assumptions:** what assumptions have you made, and what evidence supports them?
- **Failure:** how does it fail, how will we know, and how do we recover?
- **Reversibility:** how expensive is it to change later?
- **Delivery:** what is the first milestone that proves it works, and how is it rolled out?
- **Operations:** who runs it and who is on-call? What new skills are needed?
- **Cost:** what does it cost to run, and how does that grow?
- **Security and data:** what new data or access does it create?

Also use people: ask a tech lead or staff engineer to review it, and get a second opinion when the stakes are high. Notice how well the author can explain it simply, and whether they can give reasons against their own proposal.

Decide who owns the decision. In most cases, you should support the technical owner's decision, as long as the process was sound and the risk is acceptable. Step in on matters of business risk, cost, or team capacity, rather than technical taste.

#### What is an EM's role during and after a major incident?

**During:**
- Make sure there is an incident commander. The EM often should not be the one debugging, even if able to.
- Provide what the responders need: bring in people, deal with outside interruptions and shield the team from executives asking for updates.
- Help with communication to leadership and support, using the commander's facts.
- Watch for fatigue: arrange shifts and handovers, and send people home.
- Make business decisions when needed, such as accepting data delay or shutting off a feature.

**After:**
- Make sure a blameless review happens, ideally within a few days, and that it looks at system causes and does not punish individuals.
- Protect the person closest to the mistake. How you act here tells the team whether it is safe to be honest.
- Make room for follow-up actions in the plan, negotiating with product where needed, and track them.
- Communicate with leadership about impact, causes and what will change.
- Watch for patterns across incidents, and act on them. Check on on-call load and wellbeing.
- Thank people, and give credit to those who responded.

See also [How do you run a major incident and the review that follows?](../19-tech-lead/README.md#how-do-you-run-a-major-incident-and-the-review-that-follows)

#### How would you roll out AI coding tools and measure whether they help?

**Roll-out:**
1. **Set goals:** what problem are you solving? Faster delivery, less toil or onboarding? "Using AI" is not a goal.
2. **Handle the risks first:** security and legal review of data handling, licensing and what code can be shared. Publish clear rules.
3. **Pilot:** one or two teams, including sceptics, using a defined period and baseline.
4. **Build skills:** share workflows that work, and keep instructions for tools in the repository. Run sessions on where these tools help and where they mislead.
5. **Keep controls:** code review, tests and security scanning still apply, and authors remain accountable. Limit the permissions of autonomous agents.
6. **Expand** based on the evidence, and adjust the budget.

**Measure:**
- **Delivery:** lead time, cycle time and throughput, compared with a baseline and a comparison group where possible.
- **Quality:** change failure rate, defects found after release, incidents and rework.
- **Review load:** pull request size, review time and rejected changes. Faster coding can simply move the bottleneck to review.
- **Experience:** survey developer satisfaction, and where time is saved.
- **Cost:** licences and usage compared with the value delivered.

Avoid measuring lines generated, suggestions accepted or individual use, because these reward volume. Studies have reported conflicting results, including cases where developers felt faster but were not, so measure locally *(verify current research)*. Consider how junior engineers will continue to develop their skills.

### Stakeholders

#### How do you work well with a product manager, and what do you do when you disagree?

**Working well:**
- Agree who owns what: product leads on what and why, engineering on how and when, and both share responsibility for outcomes.
- Have a regular meeting, and present a united view to the team.
- Share context early: customer insight and business goals in one direction, technical risk and capacity in the other.
- Involve engineers in discovery, not only in delivery.
- Keep one roadmap, including reliability and debt work.
- Celebrate results together, and treat misses as shared.

**When you disagree:**
1. Discuss it privately, not in front of the team.
2. Find the goal you share, and identify the real disagreement: facts, priorities or risk tolerance.
3. Bring data on customers, cost or risk, and explore options together. A smaller test can often settle it.
4. Respect ownership: on product priority, the PM usually decides, while you make sure the risks are understood. On technical safety and team sustainability, you hold firmer.
5. If you remain stuck on something important, escalate together with a joint summary.
6. Once decided, both support the decision in front of the team.

#### What does your manager need from you, and how do you flag bad news?

**What your manager typically needs:**
- No surprises.
- Brief, regular updates: progress against goals, main risks, people issues and what you need from them.
- Your own recommendations, not just problems.
- Early warning on anything that might reach them through others.
- Reliable follow-through, and judgement about what needs their attention.

Ask directly how they like to get information, and what they most worry about.

**Flagging bad news:**
1. **Soon:** before it is certain, and before others tell them.
2. **Clearly and calmly:** state it in the first sentence.
3. **In a useful structure:** what happened or is at risk, the impact, the cause if known, what you are doing, what you need, and when you will update them.

For example: "The billing migration is at risk of slipping by about three weeks. A vendor API limit was found in testing. It does not affect customers yet. We are testing a batching approach, and will know by Thursday. If it fails, we need to decide between cutting the reports module and moving the date. Nothing needed from you yet."

Do not hide problems in long updates, and do not raise an alarm before doing basic checks. Use risk levels such as green, amber and red honestly, and move to amber early. A project that stays green until the week it turns red damages trust.

#### How do you communicate a leadership decision that you disagree with?

1. **Raise your concerns first,** in the right place, with reasons and evidence. Ask questions until you understand why the decision was made. You may find you were missing context.
2. **Once it is final, commit:** avoid "they decided, and I think it is wrong". This damages trust in the company and in you, and leaves the team with no way forward.
3. **Be honest without being disloyal:** you do not have to pretend to love it. "This was debated, and I raised concerns about X. The decision is Y for these reasons, and here is how we will make it work."
4. **Explain the why** as far as you can, and do not invent reasons.
5. **Acknowledge the impact,** and listen to how people feel. Pass real concerns upward.
6. **Focus on what the team can control:** the plan and next steps.
7. **Monitor and report:** if your concerns turn out to be right, bring evidence, without saying "I told you so".

**The limit:** if a decision is illegal, unethical or unsafe, this advice does not apply. Use the proper escalation routes, such as HR, legal or a compliance line, and do not simply comply. If you repeatedly cannot support the direction of the company, consider whether it remains the right place for you.

### Behavioral

For manager rounds, use real examples with names removed and confidential details protected. Interviewers will ask for specifics and how you handled the human side.

#### Tell me about a time you had to manage someone out.

**What they look for:** you gave clear feedback and real support, you acted within fair process, you did not wait too long, you treated the person with dignity, and you looked after the team.

**STAR outline:**
- **S:** [The role, the performance or behaviour gap, and its effect, without identifying the person]
- **T:** [Your responsibility, and what was at stake]
- **A:** [Feedback given, support offered, goals and timeline, HR involvement, how you made the decision, how you held the final conversation, how you told the team]
- **R:** [The outcome for the person and the team, and what you changed in hiring or in your own management]

**Example shape (invented, replace it with your own story):** A manager inherits a senior engineer who repeatedly misses agreed scope, and whose work needs major rework by others. Over several months the manager sets written expectations, pairs them with a tech lead and reduces their scope. The manager then works with HR on a formal plan. The goals are not met, and the manager handles the exit conversation in person, with a fair transition. The team's delivery improves, and the manager changes their practice by addressing similar gaps sooner.

#### Tell me about a hire that did not work out.

**What they look for:** you own your part in it, you find what went wrong in the process, you act fairly and soon, and you improve the hiring process.

**STAR outline:**
- **S:** [The role, why you hired them, and what you expected]
- **T:** [When you noticed the problem, and what was at stake]
- **A:** [How you tried to fix it, what you did in the end, what you investigated about the hiring process]
- **R:** [The outcome, and specific changes to the loop or to onboarding]

**Example shape (invented, replace it with your own story):** A team hires a strong coder for a senior role, and the role turns out to need much more cross-team influence. After coaching makes little difference, the person moves to a focused individual contributor role, where they do well. The manager finds that no interview had tested influence, and adds a structured round with rating guidance.

#### Tell me about a conflict between two people on your team.

**What they look for:** you do not ignore it or take sides too early. You hear both views, find the cause, and help them reach a working agreement. You also fix any systemic cause.

**STAR outline:**
- **S:** [The two roles, what the conflict was, and the effect on the team]
- **T:** [Your role]
- **A:** [How you spoke to each one, what you discovered, how you brought them together, and any structural changes]
- **R:** [How they worked together afterwards, and how the team was affected]

**Example shape (invented, replace it with your own story):** A backend engineer and a front-end engineer keep clashing over API changes, and both complain in 1:1s. The manager speaks to each one separately, and finds that the real problem is unclear ownership of the API contract and late changes. In a joint conversation, they agree an API review step and direct communication before escalation. Friction falls, and the team adopts the same step elsewhere.

See also [Conflict & collaboration](../../topics/10-behavioral/ANSWERS.md#conflict--collaboration).

#### Tell me about a management decision you got wrong.

**What they look for:** a real mistake, with a real cost. You recognised it, took responsibility openly, repaired what you could, and changed your approach.

**STAR outline:**
- **S:** [The decision and the reasoning at the time]
- **T:** [What you were responsible for]
- **A:** [How you found out it was wrong, how you told people, how you repaired it]
- **R:** [The outcome, and what you now do differently]

**Example shape (invented, replace it with your own story):** A new manager reorganises a team into two sub-teams without enough consultation, in order to speed up delivery. Handoffs increase, and two engineers say they feel ignored. The manager acknowledges the mistake to the team, asks for their input and merges the sub-teams, while keeping one useful change. The manager now tests structural changes against real projects, and consults the people affected first.

See also [Failure & learning](../../topics/10-behavioral/ANSWERS.md#failure--learning).

## Prep checklist

- [ ] Prepare 10–12 specific stories: hiring, underperformance, managing out, conflict, retention, growth, a missed date, a difficult stakeholder, an incident, and a mistake of your own. Use the [story bank checklist](../../topics/00-overview/README.md#story-bank-checklist-prepare-one-story-for-each).
- [ ] Have numbers ready: team size, retention, hires, promotions, delivery and reliability results.
- [ ] Write a 30-60-90 day plan, and be ready to explain what you would leave unchanged.
- [ ] Build a capacity plan for one quarter, with assumptions you can defend.
- [ ] Draft a hiring scorecard, a team charter, team OKRs and a one-page headcount request.
- [ ] Write a performance review and an outline PIP for a fictional engineer, using dated evidence.
- [ ] Practise saying difficult things out loud: corrective feedback, a difficult 1:1 and an exit conversation. Find someone who will push back.
- [ ] Practise three system design prompts on a timer, then answer the [follow-up questions](../../topics/03-system-design/ANSWERS.md#d-follow-up-questions-to-expect) for a system you have led.
- [ ] Prepare your own view of what good management looks like, with examples, and be ready to name your weak spots.
- [ ] Prepare questions about team health, leadership expectations and how decisions are made, using [14 Questions to ask](../../topics/14-questions-to-ask/ANSWERS.md#to-the-hiring-manager).
- [ ] Drill [Engineering management](../../skills/engineering-management.md) and [Technical leadership & behavioral](../../skills/technical-leadership-and-behavioral.md), and revise the [Engineering management cheatsheet](../../cheatsheets/engineering-management.md).
